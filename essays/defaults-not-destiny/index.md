The ending itself was quiet. No fight, no betrayal, no villain. A short, sweet relationship simply stopped, the way a song ends before you're ready for it to.

I waited for the heartbreak, but it didn't come the way I expected. On the surface there was almost nothing to replay. Nothing dramatic had happened between us; it had been a light, sweet romance. But even a relationship this short showed me something deep.

Somewhere around two in the morning, something stopped making sense to me. It wasn't about him. It was a pattern, a structure, and I had seen it before: in my parents' house, in my hometown, in almost every love story I grew up with.

Sally Rooney, the Irish author of *Normal People*, once said in an interview that "traditional relationship forms are drawn from power dynamics that can be quite repressive." She has also said that relationships can never be completely free of power. I read her years ago and understood her with my head. This week I understood her with my body.

Even in the most intimate moments, when two people kiss, share food, and walk each other home, the structure of society is there. You cannot see it, but it is in the room.

## The personal is political

In 1969 the feminist Carol Hanisch wrote an essay whose title became a slogan: *the personal is political*. Her point was simple and radical. What happens between two people in a kitchen or a bedroom is not only private. It is the same power that runs through laws, wages, and families, just at a smaller size.

A year later, Kate Millett went further in *Sexual Politics*. She defined politics not as parliaments and elections but as "power-structured relationships," arrangements in which one group of people is controlled by another. And she named the family as patriarchy's chief institution, the place where the larger order is copied, taught, and passed on.

I didn't come to these ideas from books first. I came to them from my own life.

## Leaving the house to see the real house

I grew up in a province in China known for its patriarchal traditions, and my family is a small version of it. My hometown is famous for its high bride price, *caili* (彩礼): before a wedding, the man's family pays the woman's family a large sum of money. People call it a sign of respect for the bride. In practice, it turns marriage into a deal between two families, with the woman as what is being exchanged. In my family, too, it is always clear who holds the money, who makes the decisions, and whose story gets told.

I never fully obeyed those rules. But as long as I lived there, I was still part of them. You don't have to agree with a structure for it to shape you. It works on you either way.

I could only see this clearly after I moved far away, both in distance and in how I think. Now I can watch how money and expectations shape my family, and I can decide how much of it I let in. Kate Millett called the family the smallest unit of patriarchy, and from here I understand why. You can only see the shape of a house once you have stepped outside of it.

## The same box, smaller

A relationship is where a family starts. So if the family is the smallest unit of patriarchy, a relationship is its seed.

I thought I had found the best possible case. He was young, kind, and well educated. His older sister had already taught him about women's equality before I ever had to. I was the one who pursued him, and I liked that. I wanted to take the lead and show him we were equal.

Still, I could see the pattern growing. I am a feminist, and he had been shaped by feminism too. We both believed in equality, and we both meant it. But belief didn't stop the gender roles from showing up. Small things settled into old places: who planned and who adjusted, who took care and who was taken care of. The structure didn't need our agreement. It only needed a man and a woman.

Now that it's over, I can see it more clearly. The relationship collapsed, so I am outside the box again.

## Power without villains

This is the part I most want to say, because it's the part people resist. Nobody in this story was a bad person. He wasn't cruel. I wasn't naive. And still, the shape appeared.

That is what I mean when I say the structure forms by itself. Power in a relationship doesn't need anyone to take it. It arrives through defaults: who has more time, who has more options, who has a safety net, who was raised to notice other people's needs, who was raised to expect them to be noticed. Two good people following their defaults will rebuild the old pattern without either of them deciding to.

These defaults are not an accident. They go back to what each of us was taught long before we met. Boys learn early that their time, their plans, and their ambitions come first, and that someone will make room for them. Girls learn to notice, to adjust, to make that room. Our generation was raised very differently from our parents'. We were told we could be anything, and many of us believed it. Yet the old lessons survive underneath the new ones, not as beliefs but as reflexes. That is why they are so hard to get rid of. You can change what you think in a few years. Changing what you were trained to do without thinking takes much longer.

## My 60/40

Here is the uncomfortable part. Even with years of feminism in my head, I slipped easily into the role of caregiver. I shared food, offered support, and did kind things he didn't ask for. He would say, "You don't need to do this." I would answer, "I want to."

Arlie Hochschild, who studied this for decades, has words for both halves of what I did. *Emotional labour* is the work of managing feelings, your own and other people's, so that things stay smooth. *The second shift* is the unpaid care work women still do after the paid day is over. I knew these terms. I did the work anyway, and I did it gladly. That's how deep it goes.

I suspect women carry some intrinsic empathy, and that thousands of years of assigned roles have amplified it, doubled or tripled. Girls from wealthy families who never learned housework show how much of it is learned. But for me, nature and nurture are not separate. They add up.

The easiest way I can explain it is with a little math. Imagine everything a relationship needs, the planning, the care, the small kindnesses, adds up to 100. I gave about 60, and he gave about 40. That is my honest number. He learned to cook and clean, and he does it. Still, the tendency to give more lives in me. I can try to push it to 40/60, but it doesn't come naturally. Being a strong feminist didn't erase the pattern. It only let me see it.

## Inside and outside

So can anyone escape? Not completely, not yet. Even when two people start from progressive places, the structure forms by default. That is my finding, and I won't pretend otherwise.

But a finding is not a verdict. Structures feel permanent from the inside, and they never are. Within living memory, women in many countries could not open a bank account without a man's signature, could not refuse a husband, could not stay unmarried without paying for it with the rest of their lives. None of those rules fell by themselves. They fell because women pushed from inside marriages, workplaces, families, and courts, and because other women walked out and built new ways to live. Both mattered. Both still do.

So I don't think I have to choose between pushing from inside and building something new. Every time a woman earns her own money, chooses her own timing, names a pattern out loud, or walks away from a deal that makes her smaller, the old structure loses a little ground and a new one gains a little. That isn't a compromise. It is how change has always moved: slowly, unevenly, and then all at once.
